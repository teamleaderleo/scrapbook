# The Rice and Beans Got Promoted

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 8 November 2026.*

For a stretch in the mid-2010s, “Want to get Chipotle?” was a proposal capable of improving somebody's entire afternoon.

We were getting excited about rice and beans.

All right, there was also chicken, steak, salsa, cheese, sour cream, and guacamole if you were feeling financially irresponsible. A Chipotle bowl could be delicious. But an impressive share of its celebrated mass came from rice and beans, two ingredients that have been feeding people cheaply for a very long time. You'd pick up this enormous cardboard bowl and think, wow, look at all this food.

Yes. Look at all that rice.

Chipotle had a wonderful way of making the whole thing feel like a personal achievement. You walked past a row of metal ingredient tubs while somebody in an apron invited you to exercise your preferences.

“White or brown rice?”

“Both.”

“Black or pinto beans?”

“Both.”

“Which salsa?”

“All of them.”

The marketing proposition was endless customization. The advanced customer discovered an even more powerful approach: **add everything**. You've eliminated the burden of choice by choosing the entire restaurant. Congratulations, your bowl weighs as much as a newborn.

And this was considered a sophisticated lunch.

## Rice and beans, but you've got taste

Chipotle occupied an extremely good spot in the food hierarchy of the 2010s. It was accessible to students, filling enough to count as a proper meal, and slightly more aspirational than going to a conventional burger chain. The stainless steel, the warm tortillas, the visible ingredients, the restrained signage, the big paper bag with the logo: everything suggested that you were making an adult decision about lunch.

The rice was still rice. The beans were still beans. A tasteful room had been built around them.

Of course, the actual food did some work. Salt, fat, acidity, spice, and a warm tortilla are a terrific combination. Salsa makes rice and beans more interesting. A generous scoop of meat makes the meal substantial. Chipotle made a convenient, fairly predictable version of this available in suburban shopping centers, on college campuses, near offices, wherever people had forty minutes and an appetite.

The remarkable part was the social prestige.

People had a *Chipotle order*. They knew it down to the salsa, and their friends knew it too. Getting Chipotle could be a plan, a craving announced to the group chat, a photograph posted online, a little reward after class or the gym. For a while, being the kind of person who loved Chipotle felt like a recognizable identity.

What a wonderful cultural feat. A national chain made putting toppings on grains and legumes feel like self-expression.

There's a very 2010s quality to it. Starbucks drinks, frozen yogurt toppings, your exact Chipotle bowl. An ordinary purchase could become a tiny public autobiography. You didn't have to invent anything, cook anything, or spend much money. You had to know your order and be enthusiastic about it.

## The beans enter middle management

Eventually Chipotle became what successful chains often become: familiar.

The novelty of standing in front of a counter and choosing your ingredients wore off. Other bowl and salad chains built their own versions of the same ritual. You could customize lunch all over the place. The supposedly enormous bowl began competing with the price on the receipt, and portion complaints gave the internet a new Chipotle hobby.

Chipotle had graduated from a destination to a reliable weekday answer.

A place you once went to *because it was Chipotle* became a place you went to because it was nearby, you were hungry, and you knew what you'd get. The special trip became the default lunch. You could eat the exact same bowl while feeling dramatically less thrilled about it.

And of course it's possible to spend too much time laughing at the rice and beans. Feeding people something filling, convenient, and tasty is a respectable business. People have been making wonderful meals out of rice and beans for centuries. Chipotle took that durable combination, put it through an American fast-casual branding machine, and gave a generation a little affordable occasion to look forward to.

I can even miss the enthusiasm. A bunch of friends getting disproportionately excited about lunch is a nice thing. The food tasted better because everybody wanted to be there, because you were twenty, because you were skipping the dining hall, because somebody had suggested Chipotle and the whole group immediately agreed.

Now the same friend who once posted “Chipotle is life” is eating a burrito bowl over a keyboard between meetings.

The rice and beans got promoted.
