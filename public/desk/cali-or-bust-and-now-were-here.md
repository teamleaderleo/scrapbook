# Cali or Bust, and Now We're Here

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 3 November 2026.*

There was a period when Canadian computer science students could summarize the dream in three words.

**Cali or bust.**

Waterloo, Toronto, wherever. Get the internship. Get the return offer. Get into the company with the famous engineering culture, the stock grants, the money, the nice campus. The promised land was California. Everybody knew what the successful version of the story looked like.

Then, years later, you actually arrive in San Francisco.

There's incredible technical talent here. There are investors willing to fund strange ideas, people building things you couldn't have imagined a decade ago, and offices containing an absolutely obscene quantity of expensive computers.

You walk outside.

Bro. Vancouver was nicer.

## The destination turns into a city

It's a funny thing to spend years treating a place as an achievement and then discover that the place has sidewalks.

Some San Francisco streets are wonderful. Others can be grimy, neglected, or depressing in ways that feel especially absurd given how much private wealth is concentrated nearby. A person can have access to gorgeous equipment and remarkable professional opportunity, then step outside and immediately miss the ordinary comfort of another city.

Vancouver has water, mountains, greenery, the particular ease of a place that can feel beautiful on a completely uneventful afternoon. Toronto has its own familiar rhythms, neighborhoods, and little pleasures. Memory adds its own warmth, of course. Home carries people, habits, and years of being younger.

But the comparison is also about the literal experience of walking around.

California was supposed to be the prize. The actual prize may be a very good job in a city whose streets you have mixed opinions about.

Wonderful. We've made it.

## Everybody arrives carrying a different life

Now for the part I like much more.

Imagine two people meeting in the same little SF professional circle. One spent their early twenties at a celebrated university, found ambitious friends, made the connections, pursued the recruiting and founder opportunities, and eventually came west.

The other took a much less direct route.

They spent long periods pursuing private interests. Drawing, writing fiction, learning software because a particular project caught their attention. Playing games with ridiculous seriousness. Falling in love. Traveling. Staying with family. Having stretches of time where the day could belong to whatever they felt like doing.

Eventually both people reach a broadly similar professional neighborhood. Maybe one has a more impressive title. Maybe the other has more autonomy. They can work together, talk shop, share a meal, even become friends.

Neither can trade away the preceding years and inherit the other's memories.

A prestigious school can offer friends, mentors, laboratories, recruiting access, and entire circles of people who will one day be useful to know. Those benefits are real. Some people find the social and intellectual experience itself magnificent.

A person who prefers large amounts of solitude would receive a different bundle of benefits. The university could be excellent, and the student might still spend most evenings disappearing into their own interests.

The name on the acceptance letter changes the opportunities. It doesn't automatically change what someone likes doing on a Tuesday night.

## What did the detour actually contain?

Career counterfactuals have a peculiar accounting problem.

Suppose the tidier version of your twenties places you in a top lab a year earlier, or at a celebrated company with a larger salary and a clearer progression. The résumé looks fantastic. You can point to the additional earnings, training, and credentials.

All right. What gets displaced?

Maybe the period you spent drawing late into the night, doing master studies and discovering artists whose work you still think about.

Maybe a romance that was wonderful at precisely that age.

Maybe whole seasons of games, with friends or alone, when the community was alive in a particular way and you were there for it.

Maybe weeks spent with family, or months of living in a different country, or an afternoon when you opened a random album and found music that would stay with you for years.

These aren't vague units of leisure that can be reallocated to retirement.

They happened.

[When Home Becomes a Visit](/desk/when-home-becomes-a-visit) is about realizing that the ordinary afternoons of an earlier life have become finite. There's a complementary realization once you've moved on: those afternoons were already worth having when they seemed limitless.

You can make more money later. You might find a better team later, or return to a particular kind of research, or start a company. The exact invitations vary and none are guaranteed.

The summer you actually lived at twenty-two has already taken its place in the chronology.

## The elite path isn't one continuous ascension

Prestige is easier to understand from a distance.

A selective university seems to lead to a selective job, then to the next, more selective room. Some people really do travel an extraordinary path. Some become researchers whose ability is plainly beyond almost everybody else's. Some build exceptional companies. Some find work they love and keep doing it for decades.

Many graduates of excellent institutions have fairly ordinary careers. They change their minds. They move for relationships. They get tired. They choose stability. They take a role that sounds dull on paper and find it satisfying. They make the best decision available and discover the next decision is still theirs to make.

Likewise, a person from a different university can find a way into the same industry, even the same team, without collecting the same credentials in the same order.

The earlier paths still affected the odds. Access is a real advantage. The outcome never belonged exclusively to the pedigree.

And sometimes you get close enough to those people to discover that you would've found the social experience exhausting anyway. You may be perfectly capable of talking to strangers, making friends, and being charming when you feel like it. Maintaining a large ambitious circle can still sound like an enormous amount of work.

You can respect somebody's college years while feeling quite pleased with your own.

## The reward has already been collected

[What Are You Saving Your Options For?](/desk/what-are-you-saving-your-options-for) asks a forward-looking question: which opportunities are worth taking while they're here?

This is the retrospective version.

A circuitous path can look inefficient on paper. Years that could have been spent gathering professional credentials were spent living in ways the person still remembers with extraordinary affection. Then, after a few turns, the path reaches interesting work anyway.

Maybe the idealized alternative would have produced a different and equally good life. There's no way to rerun it. Nor is there a particular reason to want the rerun when the actual one was so good.

The old prestige fantasy begins to lose its urgency once you've met the people, seen the rooms, and discovered what you enjoy about the work. The impressive people remain impressive in their particular ways. You can admire them without wanting to exchange biographies.

We're all here in California now, more or less. We got here by different routes. We have different stories to tell when the workday is over.

And in an especially pleasing turn, the very person who spent years declining the organized pursuit of career prestige might now be sitting in an office well into the evening, absorbed in a piece of software, having a wonderful time.

The chairs are excellent. The computers are ridiculous. The work is interesting.

The streets could use some attention.
