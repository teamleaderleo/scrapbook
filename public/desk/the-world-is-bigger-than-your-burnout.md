# The World Is Bigger Than Your Burnout

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 9 October 2026.*

Fernando Borretti's [No Man Is an Island](https://borretti.me/article/no-man-is-an-island) begins with a familiar problem. He used to love programming outside work. He wanted to read papers, learn languages, build tiny projects, write about interesting techniques. AI arrived, the professional discourse became a parade of prompts and agent harnesses, and the pleasure started going out of it.

I can see why someone would be tired of hearing about harnesses.

Then the essay makes an enormous leap. Borretti argues that sustained private intellectual life requires an external community to feed it motivation, and that AI will dissolve those communities until intellectual activity withers. He writes, "Without community, you don't get isolated individuals each working on their own things: you get nothing."

Bro, you might just be burnt out.

An interest going stale is a normal part of being alive. A person can spend years enchanted by a craft and eventually feel the enchantment leave. The part worth examining is how quickly one person's dwindling enthusiasm gets promoted into a theory of what everybody else needs in order to make things.

## You can make things and go home

Making something, sharing it, and socializing with the people who make similar things are separate activities.

You can paint alone, show the painting, and go have dinner with friends who know absolutely nothing about painting. You can write a strange little library, publish it, and never enter a programmer Discord. You can love a novel without wanting to meet its author, join a book club, or become part of a literary scene.

The artifact does the traveling.

A song can become precious to someone its composer will never meet. A technical note can rescue a stranger from an afternoon of confusion. A painting can keep a person company for years while the painter gets on with their life.

Human beings have been exchanging ideas across time and distance through things they make. Reading an old book is already participation in an enormous accumulated body of human work. It places no obligation on the reader to join the author's circle.

Of course artistic scenes can be wonderful. People make bands, start magazines, sharpen one another's judgment, compete, collaborate, fall in love. Some artists thrive in those environments. Some do their best work quietly and find their people elsewhere. Plenty move between those modes as life changes.

The point is choice. Nobody owes their craft an accompanying social life.

## The calls stopped because the questions got answered

A comment in the [Hacker News discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50025935) mourns an old pattern: friends used to call the commenter with questions about his expertise, and those calls would turn into conversations about life. Now the questions can go to AI, and the calls have dwindled.

I understand missing the conversations. An accidental excuse to talk can be lovely.

But if you want to talk to a friend, call your friend.

There are people who enjoyed being the person everybody consulted. There are also people who spent years being treated as unpaid tech support because they happened to know how computers work. For the latter group, a machine that answers the routine questions is a small liberation.

Some people are happiest spending long stretches of their lives alone. They can make things privately, read what others made, put their own work into the world when they feel like it, and socialize about entirely different subjects. They might want friendship and very little professional chatter. They might want fewer interruptions, full stop.

A quieter phone tells you something about how people used to reach you. It says remarkably little about whether friendship or intellectual culture is dying.

If you want more company, go seek it. Find collaborators. Visit a club. Host dinner. Ask somebody what they've been thinking about lately. If you enjoy a craft best in solitude, close the chat window and keep working.

**A person can be an island for most things.** Boats still exist.

## An audience can enjoy the work without joining the club

Borretti has a more serious concern buried inside the grand prediction. If public writing gets swallowed by models and returned as unattributed answers, the writer may lose readers, credit, and the pleasure of knowing someone found the work useful.

Fine. Let's talk about attribution, discovery, compensation, and ways to give interesting human work an audience.

But a loss of easy attention cannot tell us why every person makes things. Some want applause. Some want money. Some want a result so badly that they keep revising it long after anyone else would have stopped. Some want to share a finished thing and then disappear for a while.

The motivations vary as widely as the people.

And there is an especially odd assumption lurking here: if nobody needs your contribution for a practical task, nobody will care about seeing it. People watch chess played by humans despite the existence of stronger engines. They admire handmade objects despite industrial production. They listen to musicians whose performances solve no shortage of sound.

Interest, affection, taste, and curiosity survive usefulness.

## Go find another thing to love

The world's supply of interesting pursuits is absurd. New media to try, old skills to learn, people to meet, places to see, books to read, instruments to play, stupid little projects that turn into lifelong obsessions.

You can also be done with one of them.

If software has become an endless argument about agent loops and your favorite part of the work has vanished, take a break from software. Do something else for six months. Return when a genuinely interesting problem catches your eye, or keep going in the new direction.

A person can grieve the loss of a beloved activity without announcing the death of human intellectual life. Personal fatigue deserves attention on its own scale. It asks for a change in the person's life, a little rest, perhaps a new obsession.

We have more ways than ever to find things worth making and people worth knowing. We can use the new tools, keep our own company, share the work when we want, and let other people choose how much they want to engage.

And if you miss your friends, you have their numbers.
