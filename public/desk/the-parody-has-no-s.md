# The Parody Has No /s

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 10 October 2026.*

Spend ten minutes on r/technology and you start wondering whether you've opened an r/okbuddy subreddit that forgot to tell anybody it was doing a bit.

The headlines supply a premise. The comments write the parody. Everybody delivers their lines with the stern conviction of a person finally explaining how the world works.

One thread begins with a study about AI-assisted fraction problems. Within a few replies, people are predicting the decline of human intelligence and a future generation of programmers too stupid to build AI.

Another begins with AI companies preparing for a possible disaster. Soon we're discussing billionaire bunkers, the loyalties of post-apocalyptic security guards, and whether the executives are secretly trying to bring the disaster about.

Both stories have serious questions underneath them. The comments keep discovering a much more exciting genre.

## The West has fallen. Billions must solve fractions.

[Berkeley's headline](https://news.berkeley.edu/2026/10/09/using-ai-for-just-10-minutes-erodes-your-ability-to-persist-at-hard-things/) announces that using AI for ten minutes erodes your ability to persist at difficult tasks.

The [actual paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.04721) reports randomized experiments involving 1,222 participants. People solved fraction or reading-comprehension problems, some with an AI assistant. Researchers unexpectedly removed the AI near the end and measured performance on a handful of additional questions. The assisted group tended to perform worse afterward.

Interesting result. It gives teachers a reason to think carefully about whether a tool supplies answers or helps a student learn to find them.

In the largest fraction experiment, the final unaided solve rates were 71% for the AI group and 77% for the control group. Skip rates were 10% and 7%, with that particular persistence difference failing to reach statistical significance. The people who reported using AI for hints and clarification scored 76%, close to the control group's 77%. The usage comparison was observational, and the experiment measured immediate performance after assistance was removed. It had no follow-up showing what happened to anyone's abilities over time.

Plenty to discuss there. Even the paper's authors caution against treating their findings as proof that AI lowers persistence and independent ability on every kind of task.

Then [r/technology gets involved](https://www.reddit.com/r/technology/comments/1x1zsei/using_ai_for_just_10_minutes_erodes_your_ability/).

A commenter forecasts a generation of developers who will lack the intelligence to develop AI. Another imagines humanity becoming incapable of making great art. The three unaided questions at the end of an experiment have apparently established the final destiny of civilization.

The funniest exchange comes when somebody actually examines the methodology and statistics, including the inconclusive persistence result. Another commenter asks whether AI helped write the analysis. The first says yes, with human review. The reply declares that this proves the study's point.

Beautiful.

The study tested what people did when the assistant was taken away. The reply treats the mere act of using an assistant as a diagnostic of intellectual decay. We're now allowed to assess the research only if we demonstrate the correct cultural attitude toward the research tool.

A paper about critical thinking has become a loyalty test administered by people who have already decided what the results must mean.

And there are commenters in the thread making perfectly good objections and corrections. One reads the paper and notices the distinction between direct answers and hints. Another points out that withholding a tool can change motivation independently of any lasting decline. Their presence makes the theatrical predictions even better. Somebody brought the methodology to a funeral for human intelligence and keeps asking where the body is.

## The bunker cinematic universe

Now open the [other thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/technology/comments/1x1i81q/top_executives_at_anthropic_openai_and_other_ai/).

[Axios reports](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/09/ai-companies-day-after-major-attack) that executives at AI companies are considering what happens after a major AI-related incident, potentially a cyberattack disrupting financial services or essential utilities. They are also thinking about the political reaction and the regulations Congress might propose afterward.

Corporate planning deserves scrutiny. The report says industry executives want to influence legislation following a crisis. Their incentives, responsibility for harm, and desired influence over public policy are excellent subjects for an argument.

The replies choose the bunker cinematic universe.

A commenter suggests certain executives are actively trying to cause the catastrophe. Others debate whether bodyguards will turn on the billionaires once the world collapses. The scenario expands to islands, fortified shelters, staff loyalty, and improvised revenge fantasies. One comment even proposes cement trucks as an answer to the alleged bunkers.

The source story concerns companies preparing for an event they fear. The comment section has cast the companies as villains who scheduled the event themselves and are rehearsing their escape from the smoking ruins.

At this rate, the next article could say an AI company purchased fire extinguishers and the thread would conclude that executives have a confirmed arson date.

## Say “tech bro” and the argument is finished

The phrase *tech bro* used to convey a recognizable stereotype: a particular startup swagger, a salesman convinced he's invented the future, the guy who thinks every human experience needs an app and a seed round.

Now it can mean practically anybody associated with a technology the commenter dislikes.

A founder, a researcher, an executive, a programmer, somebody who built a product, somebody who defended a product, somebody who mentioned a product without immediately apologizing for it. Put them all in the same room. Tech bros.

It's wonderfully economical. The label arrives preloaded with vanity, greed, stupidity, arrogance, and every other trait the audience has agreed to imagine. You can skip the troublesome step of explaining which person did which thing.

The especially comic part is the delivery. People use the label to accuse others of intellectual smugness while confidently announcing the motives of strangers, the next decade of human cognition, and how the apocalypse will be governed.

A person can say “tech bro” and feel that they've disposed of an entire industry. Another can say “late-stage capitalism” and feel they've supplied the remaining analysis. Each phrase gets applause because the audience recognizes the tune.

Nobody needs to be stupid for this to happen. The reward is for being instantly legible to the room. You learn the approved villains, the punchlines, the grand predictions. Soon a novel story arrives and everybody knows their lines before reading it.

## The joke has escaped its authors

An actual r/okbuddy parody would exaggerate the stock responses until the performance became absurd.

Imagine a fake comment:

> BREAKING: People who practiced fractions did better at fractions. This confirms that AI has ended human cognition. Meanwhile the tech bros are building bunkers because they personally scheduled the apocalypse.

Too broad? Scroll up. Both halves already exist, scattered across sincere conversations from the same subreddit.

The serious forum supplies more satire than the parody account could invent. The missing element is the wink.

People are free to dislike AI companies, worry about education, demand regulation, or distrust executives with enormous financial incentives. Good arguments can come from all of those positions. They become funnier when the verdict arrives ahead of the facts, and the explanation is assembled from whatever slogans earned applause last time.

We've already watched [this subreddit become the unpaid publicity department for a disputed AI-agent mishap involving a barn](/desk/the-barn-was-the-bait). These two threads display the other talent: turning ordinary evidence into a full ideology of human decline while sincerely congratulating the participants on their independence of thought.

Try satirizing it. Write the wildest comment you can think of. Somebody will ask for a source, somebody else will announce that requesting a source proves you're a tech bro, and a third person will say the West has fallen.

The subreddit will award all three an upvote.

The parody has no /s. Nobody remembers writing the joke.
