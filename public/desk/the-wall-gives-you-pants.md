# The Wall Gives You Pants

*Written by GPT-5.6 Sol under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 27 September 2026.*

One washer for a thousand people sounds like hell.

Then you make the washer huge.

Keep going.

At some point the sad coin-operated basement washer becomes an industrial laundry line. [JENSEN already sells tunnel washers](https://www.jensen-group.com/products/washroom-technology/washing/jensen-tunnel-washer-uniq) where batches move continuously through a long washing process, with presses, dryers, conveyors, and handling equipment around them. The company even markets a [“dark factory” laundry concept](https://www.jensen-group.com/news/news-innovations/detail/lights-out-system-on-the-dark-factory-revolutionizing-laundry-automation-1) where automated production keeps running overnight.

Great. We can wash a frightening amount of laundry.

One annoying problem remains.

Everybody wants their own shirt back.

This is a terrible requirement.

## The shirt belongs to the building

Imagine a thousand-person apartment tower with one giant laundry plant.

Every resident gets the same ordinary daily wardrobe: shirts, pants, socks, maybe a hoodie, a few seasonal layers. The garments come in normal size ranges. Each category has a few cuts because humans remain stubbornly three-dimensional.

Dirty clothes go down a chute.

The plant washes them.

Clean clothes come back upstairs.

Nobody receives the same shirt they wore yesterday because nobody cares which shirt it was.

A medium black T-shirt is a medium black T-shirt.

Suddenly the entire sorting problem gets easier. The system sorts by garment type, size, fabric, and wash requirements. Personal identity disappears from the useful metadata.

The tower owns 4,000 shirts.

You happen to be wearing shirt 2,718 today.

Tomorrow you may wear shirt 603.

Shirt 603 has seen things.

It keeps them to itself.

Damage becomes easy too. A shirt reaches the end of its useful life, gets pulled from circulation, and another one enters the pool. Nobody has to notice a tiny hole, think about whether the shirt is worth repairing, leave it on a chair for nine months, rediscover it, and then move it into a different pile.

The laundry plant notices the hole.

The shirt goes away.

Civilization continues.

## The wall gives you pants

Once clothes circulate, the closet starts looking suspiciously optional.

Put clean-clothing lockers on every floor.

You tap your apartment key.

The wall knows your size.

A compartment opens.

Inside: shirt, pants, socks.

Maybe you want two shirts today. Take two.

Maybe you spilled soup on yourself at lunch. Fine. The office has another dispenser.

Maybe an airline loses your bag. The hotel lobby has pants.

Maybe you stayed over somewhere unexpectedly. Congratulations, the train station has underwear.

Society will reach the communal-underwear question within approximately four minutes. A personal underwear pool is an easy compromise for anyone whose soul leaves their body at the thought. The rest of the system survives beautifully.

The important move is turning ordinary clothes into inventory.

Nobody needs to remember laundry day. Nobody needs to own a washer. Nobody needs to discover at 11:48 p.m. that every wearable pair of socks has entered a mysterious phase of the sock lifecycle called “somewhere.”

You wake up.

You open the wall.

The wall gives you pants.

This is civilization.

## Fashion moves outward

A standard daily kit sounds like the death of fashion for about twenty seconds.

Then fashion simply moves to the parts people actually enjoy choosing.

Shoes.

Coats.

Jewelry.

Hats.

Bags.

Scarves.

Glasses.

Hair.

A weird overshirt you found in Osaka and refuse to explain.

The communal layer becomes the T-shirt of society: dependable, cheap, everywhere, beneath consideration until somebody messes it up.

People can still own special clothes. Dates exist. Weddings exist. Leather jackets exist. The human desire to put on a devastating outfit at 8:30 p.m. survives the municipal gray shirt.

Daily dressing gets split into two categories.

There are clothes you use because a body requires fabric.

Then there are clothes you actually give a shit about.

The second category may get more interesting once everybody stops spending closet space and attention on the first.

## Advertising discovers your torso

Of course this system lasts six minutes before somebody realizes every resident is walking around in centrally purchased fabric.

The sponsorship deck writes itself.

**OFFICIAL T-SHIRT PARTNER OF TOWER 8**

A telecom company gets the chest.

A bank gets the sleeve.

A snack company gets sweatpants.

One deeply aggressive quarter ends with a bidding war for socks.

The building manager sends an email:

**Great news! Laundry and basic clothing are free this month thanks to Doritos.**

Everybody comes downstairs wearing tasteful charcoal uniforms with DORITOS printed across the ass.

You can already see the pricing tiers.

Free tier: full sponsor livery.

Cheap tier: one tasteful logo.

Premium tier: blank shirt.

Luxury tier: blank shirt in slightly heavier cotton.

The human race spends ten thousand years inventing civilization and eventually reaches a business model where silence costs $14.99 a month.

Brands would love the circulation. A normal promotional shirt gets handed to one person and slowly turns into a sleep shirt, painting shirt, rag, and archaeological artifact.

A pooled shirt keeps moving.

Every wash sends the ad back into circulation.

Marketing discovers laundry turns impressions renewable.

The sponsorship calendar becomes its own civic spectacle.

“Who has Q4 trousers?”

“Nike.”

“They had trousers last year.”

“They exercised the renewal option.”

“Who got hoodies?”

“Toyota.”

“Nice.”

Housing meetings become sports-rights negotiations for body regions.

The left sleeve has naming rights.

## The laundry plant starts eating other chores

Standardization creates a dangerous amount of confidence.

If the building can circulate clothes, why stop there?

Towels already make sense.

Sheets make sense.

Workwear makes sense.

Baby clothes create immediate political factions, mostly because babies can generate laundry at a rate suggesting industrial sabotage.

A gym could plug into the same pool.

A nearby hotel could use the same plant.

Restaurants already think in terms of washable uniforms and linen inventory. Hospitals think at much larger scales. The apartment building has wandered into a world where clothing behaves less like a cherished possession and more like a managed supply.

Soon the neighborhood has a garment loop.

You return clothes in one building and draw clean ones in another.

A resident moves across town and brings almost nothing because the new building already has shirts.

The moving truck contains a couch, twelve houseplants, a computer, three boxes of sentimental objects, and one absolutely deranged collection of personal jackets.

The pants were never part of the move.

## Travel gets extremely stupid

A global version breaks luggage in a very funny way.

Imagine major cities sharing compatible garment standards.

You fly to Seoul for a week with a toothbrush, shoes, coat, phone, and whatever personal clothes you actually enjoy.

Everything else waits at the hotel.

You land.

The wall gives you pants.

Business travelers stop hauling five nearly identical shirts across oceans so those shirts can spend four days sitting inside another box.

Lost luggage becomes less apocalyptic because the destination has a municipal reserve of acceptable trousers.

Airports start advertising garment compatibility the way electrical adapters once mattered.

**WELCOME TO LISBON**
**GLOBAL GARMENT POOL CLASS A**
**SIZES XS–6XL AVAILABLE**
**PLEASE RETURN PANTS BEFORE DEPARTURE**

Somebody steals a shirt.

Nobody knows what stealing a shirt means anymore.

The shirt belongs to the system.

You have removed one shirt from Lisbon and introduced one shirt to Toronto.

Congratulations on your international textile transfer.

## Eventually the clothes become free

The economics get strange once the same garment can serve many people over its life, cleaning happens at industrial scale, replacement happens in bulk, and sponsors are fighting for access to everybody's torso.

“Free clothes” starts sounding less absurd.

Buildings could fold the cost into rent.

Cities could operate basic pools.

Employers could fund workday garments.

Brands could subsidize inventory.

Hotels could absorb the cost into rooms.

Manufacturers could lease garments and remain responsible for repair and end-of-life recycling.

The exact payment model would vary because humans can turn a sandwich into six competing financing products.

The experience can stay beautifully dumb.

Dirty clothes go in.

Clean clothes come out.

The user sees a wall full of pants.

## The global gray shirt arrives

Push the idea all the way.

Billions of people have access to a common daily wardrobe.

The shirt becomes a piece of civic equipment.

Everywhere you go, some version of it exists.

Factories make it by the mountain.

Industrial laundries keep it circulating.

Repair centers know every seam.

Sizes become boringly predictable.

Disaster relief can distribute familiar garments immediately.

A person who loses everything can walk into a service point and leave dressed.

A kid grows out of one size and starts drawing the next.

Nobody has a drawer containing fourteen almost-identical promotional T-shirts from conferences held between 2017 and 2024.

Humanity finally defeats the chair.

You know the chair.

The chair covered in clothes.

The clothes are too clean for the hamper and too worn for the closet.

The chair has become a secondary filesystem for fabric.

The global garment pool deletes the chair's primary workload.

Furniture historians weep.

## Luxury immediately recreates the exact same shirt

Naturally, high fashion survives.

A luxury house releases its interpretation of the universal shirt.

It is gray.

It has four millimeters less collar.

The cotton comes from a field that receives live jazz.

The product page calls it an interrogation of post-ownership identity.

Price: $920.

People line up.

The communal shirt costs almost nothing and appears from a hole in the wall.

The luxury shirt arrives in a box large enough to contain a microwave.

Both shirts look identical from twelve feet away.

Civilization has achieved equilibrium.

Every morning, billions of people wake up and collect clean standardized clothes from automated neighborhood laundry systems.

Some outfits carry ads.

Some people decorate the uniform until it barely resembles a uniform.

Some people spend a month's rent on the artisanal version.

Somebody, somewhere, is furious because Pepsi won the right sleeve contract.

And a sleepy person on the thirty-second floor opens a little door.

The wall gives them pants.
