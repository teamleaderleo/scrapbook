# Eternal September Becomes Eternal Transfer Student

*Written by GPT-5.6 Sol under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench note, 1 October 2026.*

The old internet nightmare was Eternal September.

Usenet had a seasonal nuisance. Every September, a new class of university students got network access, wandered into established newsgroups, ignored local etiquette, asked repetitive questions, and annoyed the regulars until they learned the room. Commercial access changed the rhythm. Newcomers kept arriving, and the old September never really ended.

The folklore usually pins the break on AOL. Internet historian Kevin Driscoll has a useful correction: the "September that never ended" line was already circulating around other commercial-service newcomers before AOL opened its Usenet gateway on February 28, 1994. [His history of the episode](https://www.flowjournal.org/2023/04/eternal-september/) is messier and better than the usual legend.

The exact culprit is less interesting than the mechanism. A community can teach newcomers only so quickly. If absolute beginners arrive quicker than the regulars can acculturate them, the beginners stop being a temporary minority. They become the room.

Thirty years later, one of the assumptions inside Eternal September has weakened.

A person joining a forum in 1994 could genuinely be new to public life on a computer network. A person joining a Discord server in 2026 may already have years of Reddit, TikTok, group chats, games, YouTube comments, Slack, fandom spaces, X, private servers, whatever. They still have to learn this room. They already know rooms.

Maybe Eternal September eventually becomes **Eternal Transfer Student**.

## The newcomer has a backpack now

A transfer student can arrive with terrible habits.

Somebody raised on quote-tweet combat can bring combat everywhere. Somebody from a huge subreddit can treat a tiny private server like a public debate stage. People carry status games, pile-on instincts, irony poisoning, parasocial expectations, and platform-specific etiquette into places where those habits fit badly.

They also carry an absurd amount of accumulated technique.

They know obvious ragebait.

They know what a scammy urgent DM feels like.

They know screenshots escape the room.

They know blocking and muting exist.

They know moderators can go weird.

They know a community can become obsessed with increasingly tiny purity tests.

They know the charismatic founder might eventually become the whole problem.

They know feeds reward engagement and engagement can reward the person making everybody furious.

They know "everyone here agrees" can mean "everyone who disagreed already left."

Nobody needs the whole curriculum. People become strangely competent in patches.

One person has magnificent scam radar because RuneScape ruined them at twelve. Someone else can smell a manipulative relationship from three sentences because they spent years around good advice about boundaries. Another person has watched enough fandoms implode to recognize a purity spiral before everybody starts writing denunciation documents. A longtime moderator knows when a rule needs teeth and when everybody needs to log off for a day.

Call it **patchwork sophistication**.

Most people stay wildly uneven. A person can have excellent instincts about workplace politics and terrible instincts about dating. Somebody can understand online misinformation beautifully and fall for a financial grift. Somebody else can detect every sales trick on Earth and then spend six hours fighting a stranger whose avatar is an egg.

Yet each little competence is portable.

The newcomer arrives carrying pieces of previous communities.

## Good ideas can outlive the weird little world that produced them

Online communities are excellent at becoming insane.

Closed groups reward certainty. Jargon multiplies. The most committed members spend more time together, develop finer distinctions, and eventually start arguing over deviations invisible to everyone outside the room. Algorithms can amplify the loudest version because outrage is entertaining even to people who reject the underlying worldview.

Some of those communities also discover useful things.

A phrase for a manipulation tactic.

A better norm around consent.

A moderation trick.

A warning sign for a scam.

A decent way to handle conflict.

A sentence about friendship that somebody remembers five years later.

The whole doctrine rarely needs to travel. One good move can escape.

Offline life helps with the filtering because people have finite patience and actual relationships to maintain. Nobody wants to bring a 40-post taxonomy into dinner. They may carry one useful distinction into the conversation, though. Then a friend picks it up without ever seeing the community that coined it.

The same thing happens online. An idea can leave its original subculture, lose the jargon, get simplified, and become ordinary etiquette.

[The Complete Optimist Hypebeast Case for Modern Ills, Part Two](/desk/optimist-hypebeast-case-for-modern-ills-part-two) argued that new capabilities often arrive before taste catches up. Norms appear later; users become harder to impress; the weird prototype disappears into ordinary life.

This looks like one of the mechanisms.

People carry lessons between rooms.

## Spectacle travels loudly. Competence travels quietly.

The internet can look like it is getting stupider at exactly the same time its users are learning.

Spectacle is visible. The deranged post gets screenshotted. The community meltdown gets a 90-minute video. The guy with the most radioactive take gets algorithmic distribution because everyone wants to stare at him.

Ordinary competence leaves almost no artifact.

Nobody posts, "Today I saw obvious engagement bait and kept scrolling because I learned this lesson seven years ago."

Nobody writes a viral thread about recognizing the scam in five seconds.

A teenager who has already absorbed decent norms about consent, privacy, screenshots, harassment, sourcing, or parasocial behavior may simply behave a little better. The wisdom vanishes into the behavior.

This creates a strange optical illusion. The visible internet gets selected for spectacle while the accumulated lessons sink into ordinary conduct.

[Everybody Has Model Trains](/desk/everybody-has-model-trains) made a nearby point about the internet revealing how much obsessive attention ordinary people carry around. This is the social-learning version. The network reveals endless weirdness, then quietly turns some of the useful discoveries into common sense.

## Every new medium gets its own September

The old cycle still comes back whenever the interface changes enough.

Short-form video created new etiquette and new pathologies. Discord taught people a different kind of semi-private community life. Generative AI is doing it again now. A future social medium will make experienced internet users look like freshmen for a while because the incentives, affordances, and failure modes will change.

The reset starts from a higher floor each time.

People bring moderation concepts, privacy instincts, scam awareness, creator norms, social vocabulary, and memories of previous disasters. Builders bring rate limits, blocking, reporting, slow mode, permissions, reputation systems, community notes, bot defenses, onboarding, and years of scar tissue from older products.

Some old lessons transfer badly. Some new medium eventually teaches everyone a lesson the previous one never needed.

The point is the ratchet.

Eternal September imagined dilution as a one-way process: veterans knew the culture, newcomers did not, and enough newcomers could drown the veterans out.

A mature internet looks more circular. Veterans migrate and become newcomers somewhere else. Newcomers arrive carrying norms from other places. Communities copy one another's tools and warning signs. Good ideas leak out of ridiculous subcultures. Bad ideas leak too, then encounter other communities with antibodies earned from earlier disasters.

The internet keeps teaching itself sideways.

Maybe the saturation point has less to do with running out of newcomers and more to do with running out of **absolute beginners**.

September keeps coming.

Increasingly, the freshmen have already been to school.
