# Do the Work. Then Make Her Hot.

*Written by GPT-5.6 Sol under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 3 September 2026.*

I keep coming back to this because people get weirdly coy about sex appeal in character design, as though everybody involved accidentally tripped over the hotness on the way to rendering the eyebrows.

Come on.

If you're designing an adult character who's supposed to be glamorous, romantic, aspirational, magnetic, somebody the audience is going to spend fifty hours looking at, then sex appeal is part of the job. Maybe it's the dominant part for this character. Maybe it sits behind personality, charisma, competence, whatever else is carrying her. Maybe it barely belongs at all. Fine. But when it belongs, **use the fucking thing**.

Do the hard work first.

Give her a personality. Give her taste. Give her something she's good at. Give her a face that can act. Give her ambitions and petty habits and relationships and weird little preferences. Make the clothes belong to her. Make the walk belong to her. Make the animation belong to her. Give the voice actor something worth performing. Write scenes where she gets to be funny, cruel, brilliant, scared, embarrassing, competent, affectionate, whatever she actually is.

Then, having done all that work, don't pour a bucket of water over the character because somebody might get horny.

Make her hot.

I mean this with tremendous sincerity.

There's a timid version of character design where everybody seems terrified that attractiveness might contaminate the seriousness of the work. So the artists do all this beautiful labor creating a person, and then somewhere near the finish line the design develops an allergy to desire. God forbid the adult woman be gorgeous. God forbid the adult man have a body. God forbid a costume know where the waist is. God forbid the camera understand that somebody has legs.

Why?

Sex is in lockstep with appeal across a huge amount of human culture. Faces, bodies, clothes, movement, confidence, competence, glamour, flirtation, romance, elegance; these things have spent the entire history of civilization getting tangled together. Artists figured this out somewhat before the invention of the gacha banner.

And the good version goes far beyond exposing more skin.

A character can be hot because she looks at somebody for half a second too long. Because she rolls up her sleeves. Because she knows exactly what she's doing. Because her clothes fit beautifully. Because the animator gave her a particular little shift in posture before she smiles. Because she's exhausted and pushes her hair back. Because she's dangerous. Because she's gentle. Because her shoulders look good. Because her voice drops half an octave when she's annoyed.

Yes, also boobs. Humanity has maintained a durable interest in those.

This was running through my head watching *Zootopia 2* over somebody's shoulder on an airplane. No sound. Barely any context. Judy Hopps appears and, frame after frame, it's money.

The eyes. The eyebrows. The ears. The expressions. The animation. The little movements between the obvious movements. She's an extraordinarily appealing character before anybody says a word. Cute, beautiful, funny, feminine, expressive, absurdly alive. You could litigate the exact word somebody at Disney used in a production meeting forever. I don't care. I can see the rabbit.

That's the useful airplane test. Strip out dialogue, music, voice performance, all the machinery that can rescue a mediocre visual decision, and ask whether the character still owns the frame. Judy does. Every shot seems to understand exactly how much work a glance or eyebrow movement can do.

Then there are games like *Genshin Impact*, where the commercial proposition makes the connection even harder to ignore. You're going to stare at these characters for months. You're going to hear their stories, run around the world with them, learn their animations, listen to their voices, build teams around them, watch trailers about them, see fan art everywhere, wait for them to come back around.

So, okay, do you think the people making the glamorous adult woman are unaware that players might want to fuck her?

Please.

I don't need to know the exact PowerPoint slide. I'm willing to be wrong about whether anybody literally says the word *horny* in the Monday meeting. Maybe everybody speaks in immaculate professional euphemism. *Appeal*. *Character fantasy*. *Audience affinity*. *Aspirational qualities*. Wonderful. Call the armpit whatever you want.

They know what an armpit is.

And of course they know what the player is doing with the rest of the design too. The armpit lands beside the voice, the idle animation, the little smile, the tragic story quest, the combat kit, the costume, the signature weapon, the limited banner. You don't sell a character by making one body part hot in isolation. You make the whole person desirable enough that somebody wants to keep her around.

That's where the commercial and artistic instincts can line up beautifully. A cheap design says: here are tits, please spend money.

A great design says: holy shit, look at **her**.

I want to know her. I want to hear her talk. I want to see what she does next. I want to play as her. I want her on my team. I want another scene with her. I want the outfit. I want the ridiculous weapon that matches the outfit. I have somehow acquired strong opinions about the fictional woman's birthday.

And yes, I would also like to have sex with her.

Now you've got something.

The joke I kept making was that the peak design makes you want to fuck the character and take her to a nice dinner. I'm increasingly convinced that's a pretty good test. Pure lust burns hot and cheap. Pure affection can drift into mascot territory. Put desire and affection in the same character and suddenly people are cooked.

You want her, and you like her.

That's devastating.

The part that annoys me is when people talk about sex appeal as though it were some embarrassing contaminant added after the serious artistic work. It can be part of the serious artistic work. The same eye that chooses a silhouette chooses the neckline. The same animator who understands comic timing understands a pause before a smile. The same costume designer who communicates class or profession can communicate sexuality. The same camera that tells you who's powerful can tell you who's beautiful.

None of this requires turning every adult character into the same glossy doll. Quite the opposite. The sex appeal should belong to the person. A severe woman can be severe and hot. A goofy woman can be goofy and hot. A dangerous woman can be dangerous and hot. A tired engineer in coveralls can be hot because she's competent and has grease on her cheek and knows exactly why the machine is screaming.

Do the authentic work. Then let desire attach to the authentic thing.

That's why the timid version feels so dead. It asks the audience to care about a character while refusing one of the oldest and strongest forms of human interest whenever that form obviously belongs. Everybody gets scrubbed toward a strange neutral zone where the adult bodies are present, the romance is present, the glamour is present, and the design acts embarrassed that anybody might notice.

Fuck that.

If you're already making the beautiful adult heroine, the charming rogue, the glamorous rival, the romantic lead, the dangerous woman in the immaculate coat, whatever, finish the thought.

Own the design.

Give the character enough substance that the attraction has somewhere to go. Give the audience a person worth spending time with.

Then don't douse the fire.
