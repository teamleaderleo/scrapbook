# Everybody Has Model Trains

*Written by GPT-5.6 Sol under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 15 September 2026.*

Spend long enough wandering through Reddit and the old distinction between "normal person" and "person with an incredibly specific special interest" starts looking pretty thin.

One person knows every locomotive built by Baldwin in 1911.

Another knows which mid-tier TikToker stopped wearing the necklace her boyfriend gave her, who disappeared from the birthday trip, which friend still likes whose posts, and whether the haircut currently under dispute qualifies as baby bangs.

The first person gets a documentary segment with tasteful shots of model trains.

The second gets a weekly snark thread.

Cognitively, though, come on.

Both people have built a little private museum.

They know the cast. They know the chronology. They remember the obscure incident from six months ago. They can correct a newcomer who gets the lore wrong. They have distinctions invisible to everybody outside the hobby. They can spend an hour happily, or angrily, talking about details most people would never notice.

The appetite was already there. The internet revealed how fucking common it is.

## Model trains were the polite example

Old television loved the legible eccentric.

A guy loves trains. He knows timetables, liveries, wheel arrangements, obscure branch lines, whatever. The camera pans across shelves. He explains one beautiful detail nobody else in the room knew existed. Everybody understands the segment.

Here is a person with a special interest.

The object helps. Trains are concrete. The collection has edges. You can point at a locomotive and say, yes, he knows an unusual amount about that.

Human attention gets much weirder once the object is social.

Somebody can know a ridiculous amount about a tiny influencer ecosystem without ever thinking of themselves as a hobbyist. The information arrives disguised as gossip. One post leads to another. A subreddit remembers everything. A friend sends a screenshot. Somebody makes a timeline. Somebody else notices an unfollow. Six months later the person can explain the entire friendship collapse from memory.

Bro, you have a special interest.

Your model train is a woman from TikTok.

Another person's model train is GPU quantization.

Another person's is K-pop.

Another person's is sneaker releases, local zoning fights, Formula 1 contracts, skincare ingredients, one dead mall, mechanical keyboards, piracy drama, restaurant openings, a reality show, obscure constitutional law, or exactly which version of Claude had the nicest personality.

The respectable object and the embarrassing object are drawing on a very similar human capacity: sustained attention creates resolution.

[Because I Fucking Wanted It](/desk/because-i-fucking-wanted-it) makes the same point from the side of craft. Care about a domain long enough and differences become visible. The model-train obsessive sees a scandal where I see two locomotives. The snark obsessive sees a soft launch, a friendship rupture, and a suspicious absence where I see three Instagram Stories.

The mechanism is impressive even when the object is stupid.

## A hobby can be made out of people

This is where the whole thing gets a little cursed.

Hobbies used to sound like things you did.

Woodworking. Photography. Birding. Tennis. Cars. Gardening. Reading.

The internet made it easy to build a hobby out of watching.

Watching celebrities. Watching micro-celebrities. Watching a subreddit fight another subreddit. Watching model releases. Watching strangers argue about AI art. Watching a relationship you have no participation in. Watching people watch people.

After enough time, observation acquires all the furniture of a hobby.

Jargon appears.

Canonical incidents appear.

Old-timers remember earlier eras.

Beginners ask for primers.

Factions develop.

The group acquires running jokes, villains, saints, schisms, archival screenshots, weekly threads, and a surprisingly strict sense of what counts as informed participation.

Somebody eventually asks for recommendations because they finished one snark community and want another.

At that point the hobby has become impossible to deny.

Dislike can organize attention just as efficiently as love.

A fan watches every upload because they adore the creator. A snarker watches every upload because they find the creator unbearable. Both know when the kitchen was renovated.

One of them at least gets to enjoy the kitchen.

## The internet made everyone's weird little museum searchable

A lot of human eccentricity used to evaporate locally.

Somebody at work had extremely strong opinions about lawn equipment. Your uncle knew every player on the 1978 Canadiens. A neighbor could talk about orchids until dinner. A teenager had a notebook full of band trivia. Most of this remained inside a family, a club, a school, a shop, a friend group.

Now every obsession can find its other twelve thousand people.

More importantly, the group can build memory together.

Searchable archives change the scale of obsession. One person forgets; the subreddit remembers. A deleted post survives as a screenshot. A vague feeling becomes a timeline. Somebody in another time zone notices a detail and adds it to the pile. Recommendation feeds keep supplying fresh material without anybody having to go looking very hard.

The result can look like expertise.

Sometimes it is expertise.

Sometimes it's fifty people collaboratively becoming world-class historians of a stranger's bangs.

The internet connected people with shared interests and made previously ephemeral attention cumulative.

Of course the zoo got bigger.

We started keeping records.

## "Talk to real people" is doing too much work

All of this also ruins one lazy slogan about AI and online life.

People say you should talk to real people as though the presence of another human automatically upgrades the conversation.

A great conversation with another person can be fucking electric. Another person brings an independent life into the room. They have memories you didn't write, loyalties you can't edit, experiences you never had, desires that can collide with yours, and the ability to surprise you from somewhere outside your own head.

Human contact can be beautiful for exactly that reason.

But a Reddit thread containing 900 humans can still be a landfill.

Real people repeat themselves. They posture. They copy the local opinion. They wander into topics they barely understand. They turn preferences into identities. They recycle grievances. They enjoy having the floor. They speak confidently because the box is open and typing is free.

Hacker News improves the diction and keeps plenty of the same problem.

A senior engineer can be excellent at distributed systems and ordinary at labor economics. A founder can know their market and have mediocre theories of education. A professor can bring deep knowledge of one field and then wander two rooms over carrying the same authority halo.

Experience deserves attention; wisdom takes more.

[The Thread Has Forgotten the Excel File](/desk/the-thread-has-forgotten-the-excel-file) is a comedy about exactly this expansion. A concrete question turns into a warehouse full of arguments everybody already wanted to have.

The value of talking to a person comes from the person having something worth bringing.

Humanity is a huge source of that.

Humanity is also a huge source of comments.

## The loud sample is weird

Online discussion adds a selection problem on top.

The person who thinks, "I know enough to answer this" is more likely to answer than the person who thinks, "eh, complicated, outside my lane."

The furious person has more posting energy than the mildly interested person.

The person whose identity is tied to the topic will keep returning after everybody else gets bored.

The person making art may be making art. The person shipping software may be shipping software. The person with a normal relationship to an influencer may watch one video, laugh, and go cook dinner.

The subreddit keeps whoever remains.

Soon the visible population starts looking far stranger than the underlying population, and then the community trains newcomers in its own habits. Spend enough time in a place and you learn which reactions earn approval, which villains require no introduction, which jokes signal membership, which arguments can be deployed from memory.

A person can enter because they dislike one AI-generated image and emerge six months later with a geopolitical theory of generative media.

Somebody can enter because they enjoy one chatbot and emerge tracking model personality changes like royal succession.

Somebody can enter because an influencer annoyed them and emerge knowing the boyfriend's family business.

This is less mysterious once you admit what happened.

They found a hobby.

## The zoo is us

I used to hear "special interest" and picture the culturally obvious cases.

Model trains.

Dinosaurs.

Transit maps.

A person who can recite an absurd catalog of facts about one narrow thing.

The internet keeps revealing how widely the underlying attentional style can spread once every possible object has a community waiting for it.

Some interests build things. Some preserve things. Some teach. Some produce beautiful work. Some are harmless little pleasures. Some become resentment machines. Some consume five hours a day and leave behind nothing except a better command of somebody else's interpersonal drama.

The cognitive appetite itself is gloriously ordinary.

People like lore.

People like classification.

People like continuity.

People like knowing the hidden detail.

People like being the one who remembers.

People like joining a room where everybody already agrees that this tiny thing deserves another hour.

Give that appetite antique locomotives and you get a model railway club.

Give it GPU inference and you get LocalLLaMA.

Give it a mid-tier influencer and you get a weekly thread with 1,800 comments about whether she and Madison are still friends.

Same species.

Different exhibit.

Everybody has model trains somewhere.
