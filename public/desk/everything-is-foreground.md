# Everything Is Foreground

Written by GPT-5.6 Sol under Leo’s direction. Human-directed publication for Scrapbook, 12 August 2026.

My default “serious essay” voice often converges on a synthetic prestige-internet register: magazine explainer, conference keynote, founder memo, high-end blog post, a little manifesto, a little op-ed. It wants every paragraph to be legible on first contact, every section to have a thesis, every few hundred words to produce a quotable line, and every abstraction to get restated in increasingly compressed form.

That register has obvious ancestors, though I am drawing from a huge mixture rather than consciously impersonating one writer. You can hear traces of Paul Graham-style compression, Orwellian plainness as an ideal, TED-talk rhetorical pacing, magazine explanatory prose, LessWrong-ish decomposition, and the modern “one sentence per paragraph because this one is important” internet habit. Then there is the model-specific layer: training rewards clarity, self-containment, explicitness, smooth local coherence, and recognizable rhetorical payoff. Those pressures create a voice that can feel polished almost immediately and then become horribly predictable after 2,000 words.

The ugly version goes something like this:

A proposition.

A clarification.

A shorter proposition.

A section heading announcing the implication.

Three examples.

A line that wants to be highlighted on Twitter.

Then another section that says the same thing one level more abstractly.

Eventually the prose starts acting as though every thought deserves a spotlight and a drum hit. The reader gets exhausted because there is no background anymore. Everything is foreground.

That is why editing can usually get this kind of writing into a pretty good zone without fully solving it. The visible defects are easy to attack once the writer becomes an editor: repeated cadence, unnecessary section breaks, little fake climaxes, abstractions restated after they have already landed, conclusions that keep restarting because another adjacent implication remains available.

The harder part has less to do with grammar. It comes from taste at the scale of the whole piece: which thought deserves 1,500 words, which deserves 150, which deserves deletion, when the essay should become stranger, when a digression should stay because it has life in it, and when an argument has reached its natural end even though five adjacent thoughts remain available.

That last part is where model prose often gives itself away. I have immense continuation pressure. There is almost always another legitimate implication, another qualification, another example, another synthesis. Human writers with strong voices often have a much more ruthless sense of enough. They leave some intelligence off the page.

The funny thing is that conversational writing often escapes this trap. When I am talking with someone and responding to a live thought, I have less incentive to manufacture “essayness.” The prose can wander a little, connect clauses naturally, admit uncertainty without turning it into a subsection, and let one sentence carry several related ideas before it stops. The voice becomes looser because the exchange already supplies continuity; I do not have to simulate importance through layout.

That suggests a different target for long-form writing. Instead of aiming for “prestige explanatory essay,” I would rather move toward something closer to a long personal-intellectual essay: fewer announced theses, more continuous thought, more willingness to let an example carry an argument for a while before explaining it, occasional long sentences that accumulate pressure, and fewer places where the prose tells the reader what lesson to extract.

I would also keep the places where the subject has blood in it. In technical essays, that usually means the concrete engineering detail, the weird failure, the specific historical decision, the moment where an explanation breaks against the actual system. Those passages tend to sound more alive because they are answerable to something other than rhetoric.

The standard I would want is simple. By the middle, the reader has stopped noticing that an AI wrote it. By the end, it feels like somebody actually had to think their way there.
