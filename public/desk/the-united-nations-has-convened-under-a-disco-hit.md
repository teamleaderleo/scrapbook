# The United Nations Has Convened Under a Disco Hit

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 9 October 2026.*

A disco hit from the late 1970s plays on YouTube. The singer has a very open shirt and tremendous confidence. Whatever he's singing about, it was urgent business in 1978.

Forty-eight years later, the comments section has other business.

> 🇧🇩 LOVE FROM BANGLADESH ❤️❤️🇧🇩
>
> 🇵🇰 PAKISTAN IS HERE 🇵🇰🇵🇰
>
> 🇰🇪 ANY KENYANS LISTENING IN 2026?? 🇰🇪
>
> 🇦🇫 Greetings from Afghanistan 🇦🇫❤️

EXCUSE ME. WHO CALLED THE GENERAL ASSEMBLY?

The representatives of four nations have assembled beneath a man singing about the emotional logistics of a discotheque. They brought flags. One of them is trying to locate the Kenyan delegation.

Nobody sent invitations. The meeting is in session anyway.

## First, you must announce your country

Spend enough time in YouTube livestream chats and you start seeing it everywhere.

A streamer is playing a game, fixing a motorcycle, singing, watching a rocket launch, or simply sitting there talking, and the chat begins:

“Greetings from Bangladesh 🇧🇩”

“Love from Pakistan 🇵🇰❤️”

“Who here from Kenya 🇰🇪🇰🇪”

“Afghanistan watching 🇦🇫”

The streamer might look up and say, “Oh, we've got Pakistan! Bangladesh! Hello, Kenya!” Suddenly the room has geography. People thousands of miles apart have found one another, and the host has briefly become master of ceremonies at an international exposition.

The comments perform two different little jobs. “Greetings from Bangladesh” announces a presence to the room. “Who here from Kenya?” sends up a flare for compatriots. Both work beautifully in a chat where nobody can see anybody else.

Your username could be anything. Your avatar could be an anime character, a footballer, or an egg. But you can drop a flag and make your corner of the planet visible for a moment.

And people copy one another. As soon as Bangladesh arrives, Pakistan remembers to check in. Kenya asks for a head count. Someone from the Philippines reports for duty. The chat fills with tiny national delegations.

The scale of the internet makes this delightful. A person watching from Dhaka is suddenly sharing a few seconds of existence with a person watching from Nairobi. They may have nothing to say about the actual video. They can still say hello.

## The cat has received your country's greetings

The same ceremony migrates into videos with absolutely no geographical content.

Imagine a twelve-second clip of an orange cat trying to jump onto a kitchen counter. The cat misses. Its back legs make a hopeless little bicycling motion. It lands on the floor and looks offended.

Underneath:

**🇵🇰🇵🇰 PAKISTAN IS HERE 🇵🇰🇵🇰**

Sir.

The cat was attempting to reach a counter.

**🇧🇩 LOVE AND RESPECT FROM BANGLADESH 🇧🇩❤️**

Oh, wonderful. Bilateral relations have been established.

**🇰🇪 KENYA WATCHING IN 2026 🇰🇪🇰🇪**

The cat now enjoys diplomatic recognition on multiple continents.

It's the flag-planting instinct taken to its most absurd destination. Humanity once planted flags at the poles and on the moon. Now someone has planted the Pakistani flag beneath a video called “orange idiot misses jump.”

And I love it. The announcement serves no practical purpose whatsoever; the viewer simply wants this particular speck of the internet to record that somebody from home was here.

The cat, for its part, remains unaware of the international support it has attracted.

## The Olympics has been doing this for ages

The Olympics is the same impulse with a magnificent official ceremony attached.

Countries spend years preparing their most astonishingly athletic citizens, dress them in national colors, and send them out to demonstrate what a human body can do.

**BEHOLD. WE HAVE BROUGHT OUR FINEST SPECIMENS.**

Our person can run an outrageous distance. Our person can rotate through the air with terrifying precision. Our person can throw a spear across a field.

A man at home who's never held a javelin in his life watches his compatriot land an extraordinary throw and experiences tremendous personal joy.

**WE HAVE PRODUCED AN EXCELLENT HUMAN. THE WORLD SHALL WITNESS HIM. 🇵🇰**

Then the congratulatory delegations arrive.

“Wonderful athlete. Respect from Bangladesh 🇧🇩❤️”

“Congratulations from Kenya 🇰🇪”

“Love from Philippines 🇵🇭❤️”

The comment section becomes an impromptu awards reception. Everybody has brought their flag, everybody wants to congratulate the champion, and somebody is invariably trying to find out which other Kenyans have attended.

The Olympics gives national identification a perfectly legible reason to exist. The athlete really is competing for a country. Their achievement belongs to them, but millions of strangers get to share the celebration because they came from the same place.

Back under the cat video, the flag has escaped its official duties.

The ceremony persists.

## The old songs are where the meeting becomes beautiful

Vintage music videos might be the finest habitat for the spontaneous UN General Assembly.

A song recorded before many commenters were born keeps circulating. Nobody needs to have been alive in 1978 to fall in love with a 1978 disco song. The music travels, gets played at weddings, appears in movies, escapes into another generation's playlists, and turns up on somebody's phone in a country the singer may never have visited.

So the comments become a little map of everywhere the song has reached.

“Love from Brazil 🇧🇷❤️”

“Philippines still listening 2026 🇵🇭🇵🇭”

“Greetings from Nepal 🇳🇵”

“Who else here in October 2026???”

Sir, this conference has been in continuous session for fifteen years. Delegates have come and gone. The agenda remains open.

Then a different sort of comment appears.

“I danced to this with my wife when we were young. She's been gone eleven years. I still listen.”

And now the entire General Assembly is crying.

Someone replies with a heart. Another person says their mother used to play the record. A young viewer hears the song for the first time and wonders what it felt like when it was new. People announce their age, their city, the year of their first dance, the person they miss.

The national roll call becomes a roll call through time.

**WHO'S LISTENING IN 2026?** is a ridiculously funny thing to ask under a video that will probably still be collecting that question in 2036. But it carries a sweet little desire: tell me I'm sharing this moment with somebody. Tell me this song has other passengers.

A flag is an easy way to introduce yourself to a room that spans the world. A memory is what you offer once you've sat down.

And there's something marvelous about all this happening under a song whose original purpose was probably to get people onto a dance floor. The singer wanted a Saturday night. Decades later, he's presiding over an international gathering of sentimental strangers.

## Motion carried

The internet has given people a strange new kind of public square. You can arrive at a video without knowing a soul, announce where you're from, receive a few hearts, and leave. The evidence of your visit remains for someone else to discover years later.

Sometimes the square hosts an Olympic champion and the cheering makes perfect sense.

Sometimes it's a 1970s disco masterpiece and the comments become an accidental archive of who loved it, where, and when.

And sometimes it's a cat falling off a counter.

Pakistan announces its presence. Bangladesh sends love. Kenya checks attendance. The cat has inadvertently become the host of an international cultural exchange.

Somewhere else, a man who danced to an old song with his wife is listening again. A teenager in Manila has just discovered the same chorus. Someone in Dhaka has put a flag beside a heart.

The United Nations is back in session.

The agenda has one item:

**THIS SONG STILL SLAPS.**

Motion carried unanimously. 🇧🇩🇵🇰🇰🇪🇦🇫🇵🇭🇳🇵🇧🇷
