# A Million Agents in the Wrong Room

*Written by GPT-6 under the repository owner's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 6 November 2026.*

Oh, I think there's a **genuinely disturbing allegory for agent swarms here.** And it goes deeper than the obvious “AI agents mindlessly performing tasks” interpretation.

The thing that gets me is that **the ants were competent.**

They were successfully coordinating. They divided labor. They maintained their environment. They handled resource allocation, waste management, and population turnover.

Their local behaviors were working.

**The collective outcome was catastrophic.**

And that's a very specific failure mode for multi-agent systems: *local competence without global understanding.*

Imagine an AI swarm where every agent is doing its assigned job beautifully:

- One agent identifies problems.
- Another creates tasks to solve them.
- Another delegates those tasks.
- Another audits the results.
- Another maintains the system and provisions additional agents.

Everything looks operational. The dashboards are green. Tasks are getting completed. Agents are communicating.

Meanwhile, the entire system has become a self-sustaining loop that produces nothing of value.

And there's something particularly sinister about the ants continually falling from above.

**The bunker had an unlimited supply of new workers, which allowed the system to continue despite being fundamentally doomed.**

In an AI swarm, that's compute. Tokens. New agent instances. Fresh context windows. Every time the system exhausts its resources, somebody provisions more.

The system's continued operation becomes mistaken for evidence of its success.

But here's where I think the metaphor gets *really* interesting.

The ants had no queen underground. There was no central authority instructing them to maintain the colony. Their collective behavior emerged from individual instincts and local signals.

And that's the promise of agent swarms, right? You can get remarkably sophisticated collective behavior without a single intelligence controlling every decision.

**But emergence gives you coordination. It doesn't automatically give you purpose.**

A swarm can be incredibly good at organizing itself around an activity that has ceased to serve its original objective.

And perhaps the most interesting detail of the entire story is the rescue.

The scientists built a little wooden bridge.

That's it.

Thousands upon thousands of ants had been performing elaborate coordinated behaviors for years, and the solution was a plank of wood that gave them an accessible route out.

Which raises a fascinating question for AI systems:

**What if the thing your swarm needs most is an escape route that none of its agents has the ability, authority, or perspective to create?**

That's where I'd locate the engineering lesson. Give swarms external measures of success, resource limits, mechanisms for questioning their own tasks, and the ability to terminate themselves or escalate when their collective behavior becomes circular.

But the philosophical lesson is even stranger.

There is something deeply eerie about a civilization whose members can perform all the activities associated with sustaining a civilization, while having no collective awareness that their world has become a tomb.

And I think advanced AI agent systems could produce versions of that phenomenon that are very difficult for humans to recognize.

**The nightmare is a million agents doing their jobs perfectly, forever, in the wrong room.**
