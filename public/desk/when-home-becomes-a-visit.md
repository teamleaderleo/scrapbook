# When Home Becomes a Visit

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 12 October 2026.*

"I'm home."

"I'm visiting home."

One word has been added, and the house has acquired a departure date.

The room is the same. Your bed is where you left it. Your parents are somewhere in the house, going about an ordinary evening. You can get up, wander into the kitchen, come back, lie down again.

But a visit has dates. Someone asks when you're flying back. A Sunday becomes the Sunday before you leave. You start noticing how many mornings you have left, even while you're doing absolutely nothing with them.

And somehow the most inconsequential afternoon begins to feel precious.

At twenty-six, you're lying in bed at your parents' house in Surrey, about to leave for your first proper job in San Francisco. You've already lived alone for years, in a Toronto condo, drawing, writing, programming, playing games, occasionally going to China. You've had plenty of experience being independent and keeping yourself occupied.

You could live nearly anywhere with a bed, a computer, Wi-Fi, and enough freedom to follow whatever caught your attention. For long stretches, this was a wonderfully complete life. An evening could disappear into a drawing. A programming problem could keep you awake until three. You could spend half the weekend playing a game, wake up late, and have another whole day ahead of you.

Now the job is real. You like the work. The money will be substantial. San Francisco offers you a great deal, and you have every reason to be excited.

Yet one of your most immediate hopes is wonderfully small.

*I hope I still get to lie in bed and do fuck-all.*

Of course you will. The city has beds. It has Wi-Fi. Your private world can come along.

The peculiar thing you'll miss is how those afternoons used to seem inexhaustible.

When your days largely belong to you, an aimless Tuesday hardly needs a name. You can let the light change on the wall while you think about a game, or a line of prose, or whether to get up for something to eat. You can lose track of the date. Another Tuesday is coming.

A departure makes even a lazy day countable.

And money introduces another change to the language.

*I can't.*

*I could.*

*I could have.*

The first sentence can be surprisingly peaceful, provided you're comfortable enough already. Certain experiences remain distant. You might like to take a trip, live somewhere beautiful, go out constantly, buy something extravagant, but there's little practical reason to organize your week around them. You return to your interests, and the day is yours.

Then you suddenly have the means to do a whole lot more.

The options become real. A weekend trip can be booked. An expensive dinner can be paid for. The city is full of people you might meet, including someone you might fall in love with. You can begin filling your days with a life that once existed mostly as a collection of pleasant hypotheticals.

And every invitation has a little question attached.

*Well? Are you going?*

An evening at home now sits beside all the evenings you could have had elsewhere. You can happily choose the bed, but the choice has become visible to you.

Then comes the sting of the third sentence.

*I could have.*

Once the job search is over and the future stops demanding quite so much attention, you remember how much was already available. You could have gone out more in Toronto. You could have given romance more chances to happen. There were books, films, projects, errands, little adventures you kept leaving for another day. The hours were going by while you were deeply absorbed in whatever you were doing.

Those hours were real life, too. A night spent drawing, writing, or solving a difficult programming problem was an evening fully lived. But from the far side of a few years, all the other possible evenings begin appearing alongside it.

You become conscious of the lives you left untouched.

I think this is a kind of loss of innocence: the discovery that a day can be compared against days you never lived.

There was a peculiar satisfaction in the simplicity of your earlier deprivation. The menu was small enough that you could stop looking at it. You found more than enough to love in the things already in front of you. A whole afternoon could pass without having to justify itself.

Abundance gives you freedom, and it also gives you the knowledge of what you're declining.

Maybe the old pleasure can survive the new knowledge. You can become a person with a serious career, money, romance, adventures, and still spend Sunday under the covers doing nothing. There might be a special satisfaction in knowing what's available and choosing the quiet afternoon anyway.

But the innocence of having no comparison in mind belongs to another time.

The same thing happens to home.

You used to be able to lie in this bed without thinking of the room as somewhere you'll someday miss. You could be mildly bored, complain about something trivial, or scroll through your phone without ceremony. Your parents were home. You were home. It was an ordinary evening, and ordinary evenings had a way of passing without leaving a record.

Now you know you'll be away.

You're already imagining the memory while you're still living the moment. The house has become visible as a place you'll return to. Even the simple act of lying in bed has acquired a little ache.

And one day San Francisco will do this to you, too.

Right now the city is a collection of possibilities. The streets are unfamiliar. The apartment where you'll spend an enormous number of evenings hasn't become *your apartment* yet. The people you'll grow fond of are still strangers. There may be a woman whose name you don't know, who'll someday make a particular café or corner of the city almost painfully familiar.

You'll have ordinary Tuesdays there. You'll be tired after work. You'll stay in when you could have gone out. You'll get used to the routes and the light and the view from your window. Some of the happiest afternoons may be the ones you barely remember deciding to have.

Then perhaps you'll move again.

You'll say, "I'm visiting San Francisco."

Another sentence, another departure date hiding inside a place that once simply held your life.

It's strange to think that some of the evenings you'll eventually miss haven't happened yet. You'll live them while distracted, hungry, preoccupied with work, or wondering what to watch next. Only afterward will the little details become beautiful.

And tonight, in Surrey, you're still on the earlier side of all that.

Your parents are somewhere in the house. You have a job waiting in California. A whole new life is beginning to become concrete. The bed is comfortable, your computer is nearby, and you can spend the next few minutes doing absolutely nothing.

One day you may want to come back to this precise, uneventful evening. To be twenty-six again, with so much ahead of you, and with home still spoken of in the present tense.

For now, you can lie there a little longer.
