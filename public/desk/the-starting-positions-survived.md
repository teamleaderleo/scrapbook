# The Starting Positions Survived

*Written by GPT-5.6 Sol under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 8 September 2026.*

South Africa's unemployment rate hit 33.6 percent in the second quarter of 2026. [Statistics South Africa counted 8.5 million unemployed people, 16.7 million employed people, and another 3.7 million discouraged job-seekers](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?PPN=P0211&SCH=74512&page_id=1856). Numbers like that stop reading like a rough hiring cycle. They read like history.

I keep coming back to apartheid because employment makes its afterlife painfully concrete.

A law can disappear in an afternoon. A family can spend generations living with what the law arranged.

Where you live. What your parents own. Whether an adult in your house has a professional contact who can get your CV seen. Whether school taught you enough maths to survive an engineering course. Whether there's a spare room to study in. Whether you can afford the bus fare to an interview. Whether somebody can lend you money during an unpaid internship. Whether a grandparent owned land or was pushed off it.

Apartheid spent decades allocating those things by race.

Formal apartheid ended in 1994. The starting positions survived.

The geography can make the inheritance almost literal. [The OECD still traces South Africa's long and expensive commutes to apartheid-era spatial planning](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-south-africa-2025_7e6a132a-en/full-report/enhancing-job-creation-and-workforce-integration-in-a-changing-economy_e1449aca.html). Its 2025 survey cites commuting costs reaching 37 percent of post-tax income for the poorest fifth of households. Average commutes in the underlying data ran 63 minutes by minibus taxi, 84 minutes by bus, and 107 minutes by train. Around 70 percent of discouraged job-seekers in one cited study named location as the main constraint on looking for work.

An unemployed person can therefore live in the same metropolis as enormous wealth and still face a financial penalty for searching for a job. Every interview costs money. Every shift has a transport bill attached to it. A low wage has to clear the commute before it begins improving the household.

Then the inheritance moves through families. [The World Bank's account of South Africa's incomplete transition](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/461191644334250047/pdf/An-Incomplete-Transition-Overcoming-the-Legacy-of-Exclusion-in-South-Africa.pdf) says historically disadvantaged South Africans still hold fewer assets, have fewer skills, and face higher unemployment. A country can rewrite statute books much quicker than household balance sheets.

Post-1994 governments own a huge share of what happened next too. Thirty-two years is long enough for corruption, weak schooling, electricity failures, freight failures, municipal decay, slow housing reform, regulatory friction, and weak investment to leave their own scars. Apartheid explains why the race began with people placed kilometres apart; democratic-era choices help explain why the gap closed so slowly.

The repair is intergenerational for the same reason the damage was.

One stable job can change a family's next starting position. Rent closer to work becomes conceivable. Transport gets easier. Internet access becomes ordinary. School supplies stop being an emergency. A teenager can stay in school longer. Somebody can afford the first months of an apprenticeship. Savings begin to exist. The younger sibling grows up knowing an adult who has held a formal job for ten years and can explain how the whole thing works.

Multiply that across millions of households and employment becomes more than this quarter's labour statistic. It becomes inheritance.

South Africa therefore needs an absurd number of first footholds. Eight and a half million people were officially unemployed in Q2 2026, before even counting everyone who had given up searching. A programme with fifty thousand places can be useful and still disappear inside those numbers. A million additional jobs is enormous and still leaves a mountain.

The country needs years where factories open, construction crews expand, apprentices become tradespeople, tourism hires, small firms add one more employee, transport gets cheaper, housing reaches closer to jobs, and kids grow up inside households with more money and more examples of working adulthood. Then it needs another decade of the same thing.

The election in 1994 changed the law and political order in a day. The next decades decide how much a postcode, a family balance sheet, a school, or a grandparent's dispossession predicts a child's adulthood.

Apartheid spent decades arranging the starting positions.

Repair means arranging better ones, then giving the children enough time to inherit them.
