# The Bidet Has Better Accommodations Than Dinner

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 9 October 2026.*

An influencer named Halley Kate gives a tour of her New York apartment. We enter a vast, sunlit room with excellent windows and a pool table covered in bright red felt.

The pool table is approximately the size of a rescue helicopter.

Behind it, squeezed into a corner by the windows, sits a tiny round dining table with two chairs. It looks as though the building's management has reluctantly agreed to accommodate the concept of eating.

Above the windows hangs an MTA subway sign. A real-looking one, black, white lettering, directions to Downtown, Brooklyn, Queens, all stops. Someone has taken the signage from a transit platform and mounted it over the billiards room as interior decoration.

This is a residence. In Manhattan. In New York City, where the actual subway is available downstairs.

One popular [snark thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/NYCinfluencersnark/comments/1vky9yl/) says the rent is $22,000 a month. I haven't seen a lease, so let's treat the number as reported gossip. For the purposes of experiencing this apartment, though, twenty-two thousand American dollars has become the soundtrack.

At first, I assumed the red pool table was an isolated incident.

**THEN WE SAW THE KITCHEN.**

## The casino has a cooking area

THE KITCHEN IS RED TOO.

Not a warm terracotta, or a gorgeous lacquered burgundy. A shiny fire-engine-red island with a matching red run of cabinetry, sitting directly behind the red-felt pool table.

Brown slab-front cabinets occupy the far wall. Huge stainless-steel appliances sit between them. The floors are gray tile. The stools are black. Overhead hang two dark chandeliers that look like Victorian funeral urns suspended for questioning.

There are additional glittering globe fixtures toward the side, because apparently the chandelier department conducted a hostile takeover of the ceiling.

I have seen restaurants with gentler visual identities.

The room looks like a Ferrari dealership opened a tapas bar inside a divorced man's private billiards lounge. Or a casino hotel in 2008, just after its gambling license was revoked and somebody decided to pivot into upscale casual dining.

The visual center of the home is a red pool table beside a red kitchen. The actual dining table accommodates two human beings, provided they have both agreed to remain extremely close friends.

Imagine hosting Thanksgiving.

The guests arrive. The ovens are enormous, the refrigerator is enormous, the entertaining area is enormous. Dinner will be served in shifts at a café table while four people lean over a billiards table holding paper plates.

And in the tour, Halley stands **on the pool table** to introduce the apartment.

There is something almost regal about this. A monarch ascending her red-felt dais to survey the nation. The cue ball lies nearby like an uninvited courtier.

Here's a wonderful complication: she actually likes it this way. In [coverage of her own TikTok tour](https://www.realtor.com/news/celebrity-real-estate/tiktok-influencer-halley-kate-new-york-city-apartment-move/), Halley openly embraces gaudiness and explains that the red kitchen appealed to her because it matched a pool table she already owned.

She walked in, saw the red kitchen, remembered the red pool table, and said yes. Somewhere, an interior designer felt an unexplained chill.

I respect the conviction. A deliberate casino is more interesting than an accidental one.

But my God, what a casino.

## The subway comes home

I keep returning to the sign.

The room already has a pool table, a pair of dark hanging chandeliers, gray floor tiles, black window frames, and an aggressively scarlet kitchen. Someone looked up at the remaining wall space and decided what the composition needed was public-transit wayfinding.

The MTA sign is the perfect finishing touch because it introduces another entire genre of room.

The pool table wants a sports bar. The kitchen wants a nightclub. The subway sign wants an industrial-themed Brooklyn restaurant where the menu arrives on a clipboard. The little dining table wants to live in a different apartment.

And the windows! The windows are wonderful. Big, bright, airy, exactly the kind of New York light people spend their twenties fantasizing about.

From inside that marvelous envelope, we're apparently supposed to think about transferring to the A train.

## A furnished corporate rental acquires a dachshund

The living room switches genres.

A pale gray sectional sits under a large black-and-white photograph of horses. The floor is lovely honey-colored wood. The rug has vanished, leaving a heroic expanse of polished timber around the sofa.

The atmosphere is a short-term executive rental supplied to a recently divorced regional sales manager who has been told the place comes fully furnished.

There's a little dog on the sofa, and I owe the dog an apology for involving it in this discussion. The dog has behaved impeccably. The dog can stay.

Then we enter a smaller bedroom with a brick accent wall, a white striped duvet, dark striped pillows, giant white closet doors, and a framed advertisement reading *Wiener-Dog* in cheery green.

A wiener-dog advertisement.

Above the bed.

I have so many questions about this artifact. Was it recovered from a poster sale? Does it commemorate a childhood pet? Is the bedroom a shrine to the dachshund? Why has the veterinary waiting room suddenly joined the casino?

The bedroom is pleasant enough on its own. It simply appears to have wandered in from another television program.

Then the primary bedroom arrives and announces that we've all been transferred to a Miami boutique hotel.

A huge white bed. Dark pillows. Maroon accent chairs. Fuzzy throws. A bold black-and-white rug with the energy of a zebra filing a formal complaint.

This room has a rug. The living room, which desperately wanted one, remains on the waiting list.

We've now toured a casino lounge, an executive rental, a dachshund boutique, and a Miami hotel room, and we're still under one roof.

## WHY ARE THERE TWO TOILETS

At this point, a viewer sees a bathroom containing two porcelain fixtures side by side and quite reasonably cries:

**WHY ARE THERE TWO TOILETS?**

One is a conventional toilet. The other is a standalone bidet, a separate washing fixture common in parts of Europe and elsewhere.

A perfectly ordinary bathroom appliance, in principle.

In this bathroom, however, the bidet has LITTLE GOLD FAUCETS.

The toilet, meanwhile, is wearing an emergency roll of toilet paper on its head.

Beside the fixtures stands a wheeled, tiered, black-and-white-checkered makeup cart overflowing with beauty equipment.

It's a bathroom furnished by a minor Italian prince who has fallen on difficult times and is temporarily sharing an apartment with a freshman at Arizona State.

After completing your business on the conventional toilet, you shuffle approximately eighteen inches over to perform your ablutions like a Renaissance cardinal. The gold fixtures await.

Elsewhere in the apartment is a second bathroom, and its sink appears to have been ordered from the VIP lounge of a 2006 nightclub.

**THE SINK IS A CHROME CHAMPAGNE GOBLET.**

A shiny, freestanding silver basin on a narrow pedestal. A tiny spaceship for washing your hands.

The bathroom also includes a glass shower, white-and-gray stone-look tiles, a spotted bath mat, and an ordinary toilet. The sink appears ready to present an award for Best Electronic Dance Album.

Every plumbing fixture in this home requires an oral history.

Meanwhile, two people can eat dinner at once. Three if one of them is willing to stand.

## The room for Excel, mascara, and cardio

The office has a treadmill taking up one side, a little white desk tucked under shelves, and a vanity crowded with bottles on the other.

It is technically possible to work here.

It is also technically possible to complete a marathon on the treadmill, apply a full face of makeup, and print an expense report, provided everyone involved can negotiate the right of way.

The desk has the modest proportions of a workstation assigned to an unpaid intern. A fitness machine lurks beside it with the confidence of an invading army.

Overhead sits a wall-mounted air-conditioning unit.

This little room contains more competing ambitions than an entire career-planning seminar.

At least somebody remembered books. There are actual books on the shelves. Bless them.

## And now, the rooftop recreational yard

A private roof terrace in Manhattan is a genuine luxury. Fresh air, outdoor dining, skyline glimpses, a place for the dog to enjoy the sun. An almost unfair advantage in city living.

So naturally our tour takes us outside to an area that resembles the recreational yard of an upscale white-collar detention facility.

Dark metal frames rise overhead. Tall metal fencing edges the deck. An enormous industrial wall looms behind it. The chairs and white tables appear to have been donated by a recently closed café.

I expect a guard to call out that recreation period is over.

There is a large table outdoors, which creates a delicious irony. An actual dinner party would have a better chance of being seated on the prison roof than inside the enormous kitchen below.

But wait. There's another level.

And ANOTHER KITCHEN.

The outdoor cooking area occupies a very narrow passage under a timber frame and translucent corrugated covering. There are stainless cabinets along one side, a long white worktop, and fabric curtains covering the storage underneath. Opposite it stands a bare wall close enough to inspect the masonry while sautéing.

It looks like the service alley behind a commercial seafood restaurant.

Imagine being invited over for rooftop cocktails, then discovering that dinner is being prepared in a passageway where a fishmonger might normally rinse his aprons.

You can see the ambition: an outdoor kitchen! Entertaining! A multi-level terrace! Summer evenings in New York!

You can also see the compressor, the overhead steel, the security fencing, and the suspiciously intimate relationship between the cook and the wall.

The whole setting suggests a minimum-security facility where white-collar inmates may enjoy two glasses of Chardonnay each evening.

The air-conditioning equipment is the most competent visual element in the picture. It has a purpose, announces it honestly, and performs its job without asking anyone to admire its personality.

## Twenty-two thousand dollars and an identity crisis

The apartment itself has excellent possibilities. Big windows, tons of light, exposed brick, beautiful wood flooring in places, a substantial kitchen, multiple rooms, outdoor space, genuinely lavish appliances. There is plenty here to admire.

And I enjoy maximalism. Give me the weird chandelier. Give me a red kitchen when the color is used with enough confidence. A person who wants theatrical interiors should have theatrical interiors.

Halley's own enthusiasm makes the whole tour much funnier, and also a little more endearing. She is gleefully showing us exactly what she wanted.

The problem is that each room seems to have been decorated by a separate committee operating without email.

A pool hall wants to impress a casino.

A corporate rental wishes to appear respectable.

A dachshund has acquired a modest bedroom.

A Renaissance cardinal has secured excellent bathroom privileges.

A nightclub in 2006 has donated its sink.

A prison architect has been permitted to advise on outdoor entertaining.

The only person who appears to have lost every negotiation is the dining table.

Maybe the reported rent is wrong. Maybe the new furniture will eventually reconcile these competing civilizations. Maybe the subway sign will develop a charming friendship with the Gothic chandeliers and everybody will live together in peace.

For now, this is the apartment in the screenshots, and I can only marvel.

**The pool table occupies the ceremonial center of the home. The bidet has its own gold-tapped station. The kitchen can cater a small banquet. Dinner seats two.**

And somewhere on the rooftop, the air-conditioning compressor is quietly doing the one thing everyone agrees it came here to do.
