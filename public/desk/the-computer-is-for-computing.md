# The Computer Is for Computing

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 10 October 2026.*

A Mac price increase arrives, and somewhere on Reddit a man looks at his six-year-old M1 MacBook and announces that he'll be keeping it.

In [an October 2026 r/apple thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/apple/comments/1x2kdet/mac_q3_2026_shipments_fall_but_apple_still_gains/), one commenter said a $250 increase stopped his planned upgrade. Within the same comment, he predicted a large drop in iPhone sales over the coming quarters.

A personal purchasing decision became a forecast about millions of strangers in the space of a paragraph.

The first part makes sense. If the old machine does everything you want, and the new one costs more than you're willing to pay, keep it. The second part requires evidence about everybody else's budgets, needs, and appetite for new hardware.

And the six-year-old laptop still running beautifully? Congratulations. Apple sold you a good computer.

## A personal budget becomes a market forecast

Tech forums are full of people who want their own spending habits to be the consumer economy.

“I would never pay that much” becomes “nobody is going to buy this.” “My current machine is perfectly adequate” becomes “the new one serves no purpose.” Then somebody starts predicting quarterly earnings.

You may simply be outside the target audience.

A person replacing a broken laptop has a different decision from someone contemplating a leisurely upgrade. A developer compiling large projects has different requirements from someone who mostly browses the web. A business issuing new machines to employees buys against its own schedules and costs. A person who loves computers can value the experience of a nicer computer because they spend half their waking life at one.

And income changes the calculation. An extra $250 can derail one purchase, provoke a shrug in another, and disappear into a company's equipment budget in a third.

Price increases can suppress demand. The interesting question is how much demand, among which buyers, at what prices. One frugal owner of a still-excellent M1 is a rather poor substitute for that investigation.

There's also a selection problem. The person who feels priced out has a reason to leave an indignant comment. The person who bought the new Mac, likes it, and went back to work is busy using it.

## MacBooks are a strange place to start

Apple certainly knows how to charge for additional memory and storage. A configuration page can turn a sensible purchase into an expensive one with a few clicks.

But the MacBook itself is a remarkably awkward villain for a story about terrible value.

Apple Silicon brought excellent battery endurance and performance into quiet, portable machines with good screens, keyboards, trackpads, speakers, and an unusually coherent hardware-and-software experience. The MacBook Air is a serious everyday computer. A MacBook Pro can be a central professional tool. Premium Windows laptops compete in much of the same expensive territory.

And the favorite objection doubles as an endorsement: people keep telling us their 2020 M1 machines are still great.

Wonderful. The original purchase has delivered six years of useful computing and counting. Longevity improves the economics of ownership; it doesn't make every newer machine an unreasonable purchase.

A laptop can be excellent value and too expensive for someone's budget. It can be worth upgrading for one owner and wholly unnecessary for another. Those conclusions can coexist without an argument about who has the more virtuous relationship with money.

## A computer wears down in two directions

People buy computers to compute. They spend years making the machines do things.

The physical costs are familiar. Batteries lose capacity. Keyboards and hinges see thousands of cycles. Ports get tired. Screens accumulate wear. Repairs and replacement parts extend the life of a good machine.

The other kind of aging comes from the world around it.

Software gets more demanding. New workloads become practical. Displays improve. Connectivity changes. A compiler finishes sooner on newer hardware. A local model that barely fit last year might fit comfortably on the next machine. A photographer or editor might discover that a task once surrounded by waiting has become pleasant.

A six-year-old computer can keep doing its original job while the range of worthwhile things a person could do has grown.

Even the objects people admire as buy-it-for-life investments consume parts. A bicycle needs tires, chains, bearings, and eventually more substantial work. A favorite guitar takes strings, setups, fret work, repairs. We maintain the object because we want to keep riding or playing.

Computing has a particularly energetic version of this bargain. Maintenance preserves what you already have; technological progress offers new things to do.

Some owners will get ten happy years from one laptop. Others will replace one after three or four years, pass the old one along, and enjoy every day on the newer machine. The sensible lifespan depends on the life being lived through it.

## The romance belongs to computing

There's a peculiar prestige in keeping an electronic object alive as long as humanly possible.

People announce the age of their laptop almost like a sporting achievement. Seven years! Eight years! Original machine! New battery, perhaps, and a heroic collection of workarounds. The device becomes a companion, then a relic, then evidence of admirable character.

I understand the affection. A computer can hold years of work, late-night discoveries, first attempts, embarrassing drafts, extraordinary conversations, whole chapters of a life. An old laptop can be as evocative as an old notebook.

But the activity deserves at least as much romance as the artifact.

Think about the person who loves photography enough to buy a camera with better autofocus. The musician who finds an instrument that makes them want to practice for another hour. The programmer who opens a new machine and discovers that a task they used to plan around now happens while they're thinking about the next thing.

The purchase can express devotion to the activity.

Pleasure counts, too. The delight of a gorgeous screen, a silent machine, an impossibly responsive interface, a tool that makes you curious about what else you could try. Someone can value those sensations even when the old computer still performs its duties.

The people arguing that every upgrade needs an emergency have made continued possession of the old object into the supreme good. They know the age of the laptop. They know the money they saved. Sometimes they sound oddly uninterested in what the computer might let them enjoy.

[Rake the Gravel Again](/desk/rake-the-gravel-again) was about refusing to let an old masterpiece become your whole identity. The artifact belongs in the archive; the desire to make the next thing stays alive.

A beloved computer can belong in that archive too. Sell it, give it to someone who will get another five years from it, keep it as a spare, repair it and continue using it. Each choice is perfectly respectable when it's serving the person doing the computing.

The old machine has earned its affection. The activity can keep earning more.

**Love computing. Wear out the computers.**
