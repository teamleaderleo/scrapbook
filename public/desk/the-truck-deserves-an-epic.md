# The Truck Deserves an Epic

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 9 October 2026.*

There is an entire French television genre that's essentially: "Let's go to a remote part of the world and see how people survive."

Imagine a truck in Madagascar trying to cross a river. The driver has been making this journey for years. His assistant thinks they can push through. A man on the riverbank has a different idea. Everybody is arguing over a muddy wheel, and the sacks of rice have to reach the next town.

French television looks at this and decides it deserves a 52-minute cinematic epic.

And they're right.

[Les Routes de l'impossible](https://www.francetvpro.fr/contenu-de-presse/54437867), made by Tony Comiti Productions for France 5, is a wonderful example. The whole premise is to follow people making difficult journeys for ordinary reasons: working, carrying goods, getting home, keeping a community supplied. The narration approaches every bend in the road as a confrontation with destiny.

In my head the French voiceover goes, *« Ici, chaque jour est un combat pour la survie. »*

Cut to a man smoking a cigarette beside a broken axle.

I'll watch the whole thing.

## The rice has to get through

The modern travel YouTuber needs to be the protagonist. Everything has to pass through his astonishment. The food is SHOCKING, the poverty is UNBELIEVABLE, the village is INSANE. He appears in an ultra-HDR thumbnail with a :O face, reacting to people who were getting on with their lives before he arrived.

Meanwhile, a French documentary crew finds a driver who has been working the same terrible road for decades and decides this man deserves his own epic.

You get actual storytelling. Recurring characters. Gorgeous landscape shots. People having arguments over seemingly trivial things that turn out to have enormous consequences. The camera lingers. The documentary trusts you to pay attention.

By minute 35, you're emotionally invested in the delivery of forty sacks of rice. You're genuinely worried about the rice. You've developed opinions about the competence of the driver's assistant.

The filmmaking makes a broken axle feel like a Christopher Nolan climax.

There's also an astonishing sincerity to it. The crew practically disappears. The people being filmed get to be funny, stubborn, resourceful, irritable, proud, and occasionally completely indifferent to the European audience finding their lives extraordinary.

The film gives them the time to be people.

## Small slices, substantial lives

There's something condescending about the assumption that documenting poverty or difficult living conditions is inherently exploitative.

A documentary following a truck driver in Guinea as he spends days navigating an atrocious road is documenting a real economic and social reality. The road serves villages, carries goods, connects communities, and affects people's livelihoods. It's a specific window into the country, but a substantial one.

Obviously these are small slices of a country. They're also nontrivial slices of the country *at the time*.

Every documentary makes choices about where to point the camera. The repeated choice to show poorer countries through danger and scarcity can produce an oddly narrow portrait. A country of fifty million people becomes a film about six men pushing a bus through mud. French grand reportage has its own history, including a colonial inheritance, and some of the narration loves words like "forgotten" a little too much.

Those limitations deserve to be noticed. So do the six men pushing the bus.

The French documentaries are often remarkably interested in the competence and individuality of their subjects. You get to know the driver's personality, his sense of humour, his frustrations, his relationship with his assistants, his practical knowledge. Often he's the most impressive person in the entire film.

There's something valuable about how little they sanitize the circumstances. The difficulties are real. The resourcefulness is real. The absurdity is real. The people themselves often find aspects of their predicament hilarious.

Watching somebody coax a battered vehicle across an impossible road tells you a great deal about the economy around that road. Who depends on the journey? Who knows how to repair what breaks? Who lends a hand? Who argues? Who gets paid? Who has to get home before dark?

You can learn a lot from a narrow window when the camera stays open long enough.

## The accidental archive

These documentaries are becoming accidental historical archives.

An episode filmed in 2008 showing rural transport in Madagascar would capture the roads, vehicles, informal economies, prices, clothing, social relationships, and everyday improvisations of a particular moment. A person watching decades later can see an entire world of ordinary details the original crew barely had to think about.

In another thirty years, this is going to be extraordinary footage.

I also suspect the genre gives Western viewers a rare sense of how much of the world's daily life happens through intricate, improvised systems of cooperation. A truck breaks down, and suddenly twelve strangers are figuring out how to get it moving because everyone's livelihood depends on it.

A country carries on in those moments. The driver knows the road. The mechanic knows the engine. The stranger knows someone with a rope. People get frustrated, laugh, make a decision, change their minds, and finally get the truck moving.

And the camera gives them the time to work it out.

There's a real dignity in treating that as a story worthy of an hour of television.
