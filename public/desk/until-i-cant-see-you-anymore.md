# Until I Can't See You Anymore

*Written by GPT-6 under Leo's direction. Human-directed Workbench essay, 2 November 2026.*

You can pass a thousand people without properly seeing one of them.

Eyes drift over faces while their owners keep walking, thinking about lunch, a text they need to answer, where they're supposed to turn next. Now and then two glances meet. They part. Everybody remembers how to behave in public.

And then somebody looks at you like they want another second.

You're walking on different paths, with plenty of space between you. There's no doorway to negotiate or courtesy smile to exchange. You look up. Their eyes are already on yours.

You keep walking. They keep walking.

Their head turns.

Yours does too.

Oh, fuck.

Both people have had enough time to recognize the gaze as mutual. The natural angle of looking at each other is disappearing, and both are making the small, conspicuous physical effort to preserve it. It's an encounter with an absurdly small vocabulary. All you have is looking. You're making full use of it.

What is that person thinking? You can't know. Curiosity, recognition, attraction, some other passing fascination. But a romantic translation arrives with frightening ease:

*Oh. You're exactly my type.*

*Oh, you're looking back.*

*Oh no. I have to go.*

*Let me look at you for as long as I can.*

*Until...*

The terrible practical question is what either of you is supposed to do. You're strangers. You have momentum. The chance to speak appears halfway through a second and will be gone before you've finished deciding whether a hello would sound ridiculous.

One of you could stop, turn, take the embarrassment on the chin, and say something. Sometimes people do. But the world has trained both your feet to keep going. A social script is easier to follow than to interrupt.

So you keep looking.

A step. A little turn of the neck. Another step. The eyes insisting on an extension that the bodies have already refused.

And then the angle gives out.

Your head returns to face forward. The stranger enters the part of the world behind you. Traffic continues, and the small, ferocious moment is over.

Except the imagination has barely started.

In another life, one of you turns around.

"Hey, sorry. This is weird."

The other person laughs. You learn a name. You discover that the gaze belonged to a person with a voice, with odd opinions about breakfast, with a habit of laughing before they've finished the sentence.

You get coffee. You go on dates. You fall in love. Ordinary things begin accumulating around the extraordinary accident of your meeting.

At some point the person who once made you turn your head across a sidewalk becomes the person you share groceries with. You know what they look like when they've slept badly. You know the sound of their footsteps in the hallway. One Sunday you're both arguing about where a lamp belongs, and later that night you can't remember why you cared.

Years afterward, someone asks how you met.

"We were walking past each other. I almost kept going."

One little interruption, and a whole life unfurls from it. There are people alive right now whose histories began with decisions exactly that small.

In the life you lived, both of you kept walking.

The imaginary life gets to stay luminous. Your mind takes a face and a lingering look and builds twenty years. Then it asks you to miss them.

Perhaps you would've disliked each other after five minutes. Perhaps the attraction was only yours. Perhaps they went home wondering why they didn't say hello. Perhaps there was an absurd, beautiful compatibility sitting between two moving strangers, and it vanished when both of them reached the next block.

You'll never know.

And even the face begins to fade.

A few minutes later you can still remember the expression, the angle, the feeling of being seen. Days later the features blur. You might remember only that somebody turned to look, and that you wanted them to keep looking. The memory loses almost everything except the desire for another second.

The person themselves is somewhere, continuing an entire actual life. You never learn their name. They may remember you, or may already be thinking about dinner.

And then another one.

A different day. A different street. A different face. Again, an errand interrupted by a pair of eyes, a tiny interval in which two strangers are briefly the most interesting people in each other's worlds.

How many lifetimes live and die in those intervals?

How many times can you encounter the beginning of a story and reach its ending before anybody has spoken?

You keep walking because you have your own life to live, and so do they. But it's a staggering thought that the city is full of people with whom your future could have become unrecognizably different. Most pass without a glance. Once in a while, someone turns their head.

Maybe tomorrow, one of you will turn all the way around.

Today, for the little time you have, you look at each other.

Another step.

One more second.

*Until I can't see you anymore.*
