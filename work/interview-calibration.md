# Interview calibration

This record preserves one important correction to how past interview outcomes should be interpreted.

## Current observation

Leo has demonstrated that when an evaluation target is legible and worth preparing for, he can prepare very aggressively and perform well under conventional testing/interview conditions.

Relevant evidence includes:

- passing Google's L3 technical interview bar strongly enough to proceed through an extended team-matching process;
- historically very strong standardized-test performance (including a 1560/1600 SAT) when the target and preparation path were clear;
- repeated recent engineering work showing the same pattern outside tests: once a problem becomes concrete enough to investigate, effort and iteration can become unusually intense.

This does **not** prove that every future interview will go well, nor that standardized testing predicts day-to-day engineering performance. It changes the interpretation of older interview misses.

## Working interpretation

Past interview outcomes should not automatically be treated as a stable estimate of Leo's technical ceiling or interview ability.

A more plausible model is preparation-sensitive performance:

> When the target is clear, the preparation path is concrete, and Leo decides the opportunity matters, study intensity can become very high and interview/test performance can move substantially.

Under that model, an earlier failure can mean several different things:

- insufficient preparation;
- uncertainty about what the process would actually test;
- low motivation for the specific opportunity;
- excessive friction in finding or organizing the right study material;
- a genuine weakness exposed by the instrument.

The last category matters, but it should be separated from the first four instead of assuming every miss was a capability verdict.

## Consequence for job fit

Interview-process transparency is itself a fit variable.

A process is easier to prepare for when the company clearly communicates:

- interview stages and formats;
- what technical dimensions are evaluated;
- representative examples or preparation material;
- whether coding is algorithmic, practical, codebase-based, debugging-oriented, or system-design-heavy;
- tool/AI policy;
- expected depth versus breadth.

This does not mean Leo should avoid opaque processes. It means transparent processes reduce unnecessary variance and let preparation intensity become an asset.

## Consequence for preparation

Do not infer "I am bad at this interview type" from a small number of underprepared attempts.

For a role that matters:

1. identify the actual interview instrument as early as possible;
2. make the study surface finite and concrete;
3. build drills that resemble the instrument rather than studying software engineering generically;
4. track errors by mechanism, not by emotional impression;
5. retest after focused preparation before drawing a ceiling conclusion.

For algorithmic interviews, this means a bounded pattern/problem bank and timed reps. For systems/performance interviews, it means concise project explanations, tradeoff drills, debugging exercises, and conventional vocabulary. For behavioral interviews, it means selecting real stories and practicing compression rather than inventing polished narratives on the spot.

## What this should not become

Do not use strong Google/SAT outcomes to claim universal exceptional ability.

The useful claim is narrower:

> There is strong evidence that Leo can perform well on demanding structured evaluations when the target is understood and he chooses to train for it.

That makes preparation quality a controllable variable in future interview outcomes rather than treating the entire process as an opaque personality test.
